1.3 Central Theme

The lesson explores:

  • risk;
  • personal decisions;
  • everyday risk-taking;
  • extreme sports;
  • safety;
  • consequences;
  • conditional situations;
  • decision-making;
  • arguments for and against;
  • experiences involving risk.

The central communicative question is:

How do people make decisions when there is risk involved?

1.4 Main Grammar

The grammar focus is:

Zero Conditional

Used for situations presented as generally true, habitual, or predictable.

Basic pattern:

If + present simple,
present simple

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you run the risk of paying expensive medical fees.

First Conditional

Used for a possible future condition and its consequence.

Basic pattern:

If + present simple,
will + base verb

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you will have to pay for the treatment yourself.


The unit also explicitly works with **future time clauses**.

Typical connectors include:

- when;
- as soon as;
- until;
- unless;
- before;
- after.

The important teaching point is that future time clauses can refer to the future while using a present form in the time clause.

STEP 2 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES

2.1 Main Aim

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to discuss risk-taking and risky situations using:

  • zero conditional;
  • first conditional;
  • future time clauses;
  • risk-related vocabulary;
  • expressions with take;
  • linked phrases in natural speech;
  • arguments for and against.

2.2 Grammar Objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. recognize zero conditional;
  2. recognize first conditional;
  3. distinguish their meanings;
  4. form both accurately;
  5. understand when the result is presented as generally true;
  6. understand when the result is presented as a possible future consequence;
  7. use future time clauses correctly;
  8. avoid using will incorrectly inside the future time clause;
  9. produce original conditional sentences;
  10. use conditionals in meaningful risk-related communication.

2.3 Pronunciation Objectives

Students will:

  1. notice linking between words;
  2. identify connected speech in the model phrases;
  3. understand how linking affects the sound of a phrase;
  4. practice saying phrases without unnecessary pauses;
  5. transfer linking into spontaneous speech.

2.4 Vocabulary Objectives

Students will:

  1. understand key extreme-sport vocabulary;
  2. understand common expressions with take;
  3. distinguish literal take from multi-word expressions;
  4. use selected expressions in new sentences;
  5. retrieve the expressions without the textbook;
  6. use them in speaking and writing.

2.5 Reading Objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. identify extreme sports from visual information;
  2. predict article content;
  3. read for the writer's main reasons;
  4. locate names and numerical information;
  5. interpret the final paragraph;
  6. discuss the issue critically.

2.6 Listening Objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. predict content;
  2. listen for gist;
  3. identify speakers and topics;
  4. listen for specific information;
  5. identify attitudes toward risk;
  6. use information from the listening in later discussion.

2.7 Speaking Objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. describe their own attitude to risk;
  2. ask and answer questions about risky behavior;
  3. compare levels of risk;
  4. justify opinions;
  5. describe experiences;
  6. use conditional language;
  7. discuss extreme sports;
  8. express agreement/disagreement;
  9. maintain a discussion.

2.8 Writing Objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. understand the purpose of a for-and-against text;
  2. distinguish supporting and opposing arguments;
  3. organize ideas logically;
  4. use linking expressions;
  5. write a balanced conclusion;
  6. use relevant vocabulary and grammar.

2.9 Higher-Level Objective

The deeper objective is:

Students should learn to use English to evaluate risk, explain consequences, and express a balanced position.

The lesson is therefore not simply:

"Learn conditionals."

It is:

Use conditional language to reason about possible consequences.

STEP 4 — DETAILED PROCEDURE

STAGE 1 — RISK ACTIVATION

Time

7 minutes

Objective

Activate the concept of risk before students encounter the textbook listening.

Interaction

T → Ss
Ss → pairs

Step 1 — Board Prompt

Write:

RISK

Ask:

What does "risk" mean?

Do not immediately give a dictionary definition.

Elicit examples.

Step 2 — Personal Scale

Write:

1 = not risky at all
5 = very risky

Ask students to rate several activities from the textbook topic.

Possible examples include:

  • riding a bike in your city;
  • smoking;
  • buying a used car;
  • hiking in the mountains;
  • online dating;
  • eating street food while traveling;
  • cosmetic surgery;
  • telling a lie on your résumé.

Step 3 — Compare

Students compare:

Which activity is most risky for you?

Which is least risky?

Why?

Teacher Monitoring

Listen for:

  • vocabulary gaps;
  • strong opinions;
  • conditional language;
  • useful expressions.

Do not interrupt frequently.

Transition

Say:

Let's hear what other people think about risk.

STAGE 2 — LISTENING — RISK-TAKING

Time

12 minutes

Objective

Develop listening for gist and specific information while introducing different attitudes toward risk.

PRE-LISTENING

Ask:

What makes something risky?

Possible dimensions:

physical danger
financial danger
social consequences
legal consequences
emotional consequences

Ask:

Can something be risky without being physically dangerous?

Expected:

Yes.

FIRST LISTENING

Students listen to the four speakers.

Task:

Identify the general topic of each speaker.

Do not ask students to understand every word.

SECOND LISTENING

Students complete the textbook classification / information task.

The four speakers are:

  • Holly;
  • Natalie;
  • Tom;
  • Jeanie.

Students identify what type of risk each speaker is discussing.

THIRD LISTENING / DETAIL

Students identify the specific statements required by the textbook.

Encourage them to listen for:

  • changes in attitude;
  • decisions;
  • emotional reactions;
  • perceived value of risk;
  • usefulness of risky experiences.

PAIR CHECK

Students compare answers.

Instruction:

Explain your answer to your partner.

WHOLE-CLASS FEEDBACK

Do not only say:

Correct.

Ask:

What did you hear?

Which words helped you?

This trains evidence-based listening.

STAGE 3 — SPEAKING — PERSONAL RISK

Time

10 minutes

Objective

Move from listening about other people's risk to students' own attitudes.

Step 1 — Textbook Interview

Students work in pairs.

Student A interviews Student B using the risk questions.

The textbook organizes the discussion around areas such as:

Appearance
On the road
Shopping and money
Food
Travel
Work and study

Step 2 — Risk Classification

Students decide:

Would I take this risk?

Mark:

R = I would take the risk
N = I would not take the risk

Step 3 — Justification

Students must explain:

Why?

Encourage:

because...

because of...

if...

unless...

This naturally prepares the grammar stage.

Step 4 — Compare

Ask:

Who is the bigger risk-taker?

Students compare their answers.

STAGE 4 — GRAMMAR

Zero and First Conditionals + Future Time Clauses

Time

18 minutes

Objective

Students understand and use the conditional structures as tools for reasoning about risk.

PART A — NOTICE

Use examples from the textbook grammar section.

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you run the risk of paying expensive medical fees.

Ask:

Is this presented as a general consequence or one specific future prediction?

Guide students toward:

general consequence.

PART B — ZERO CONDITIONAL

Board:

IF + PRESENT SIMPLE
+
PRESENT SIMPLE

Concept:

a situation and its usual / general consequence.

Timeline / Concept

Situation
   ↓
usual consequence

IF X HAPPENS
      ↓
Y HAPPENS

CCQs

Is this about one specific future event?

Not necessarily.

Can it describe something that generally happens?

Yes.

Is the result presented as a normal consequence?

Yes.

PART C — FIRST CONDITIONAL

Board:

IF + PRESENT SIMPLE
+
WILL + BASE VERB

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you will have to pay for the treatment yourself.

Meaning

A possible condition in the future and its possible result.

CCQs

Is the situation possible in the future?

Yes.

Is the result certain?

No.

Does the speaker see the result as a possible consequence?

Yes.

PART D — CONTRAST

Board:

ZERO CONDITIONAL

If X happens,
Y happens.

GENERAL / HABITUAL / PREDICTABLE


FIRST CONDITIONAL

If X happens,
Y will happen.

POSSIBLE FUTURE

PART E — IMPORTANT FORM RULE

The if-clause normally uses the present simple even when the meaning is future.

Correct:

If I go abroad, I will buy travel insurance.

Not:

If I will go abroad, I will buy travel insurance.

PART F — FUTURE TIME CLAUSES

Introduce:

  • when;
  • as soon as;
  • until;
  • unless;
  • before;
  • after.

Example:

When I arrive, I'll call you.

Not:

When I will arrive, I'll call you.

CCQ

Does "when I arrive" refer to the future?

Yes.

Does the clause use present simple?

Yes.

PART G — TEXTBOOK CONTROLLED PRACTICE

Students complete the matching exercise.

Then:

  1. compare in pairs;
  2. justify the choices;
  3. teacher checks.

PART H — PERSONALIZED PRACTICE

Students complete conditional prompts.

Examples:

Don't buy a used car unless...

You shouldn't think about getting a tattoo if...

Keep a first-aid kit in your house in case...

Children shouldn't use social media until...

Always take out travel insurance in case...

STAGE 5 — PRONUNCIATION

Linked Phrases

Time

8 minutes

Objective

Help students hear and produce connected speech in natural phrases.

Step 1 — Listen

Students listen to the model phrase.

Step 2 — Notice

Ask:

Which words seem to connect?

Step 3 — Visual Marking

Teacher marks linking:

word | word | word
      ↓
connected speech

Step 4 — Slow → Natural

Students say the phrase:

slowly
 ↓
normally
 ↓
naturally / quickly

Step 5 — Personalize

Students create one sentence using the same rhythm / linking pattern.

Teaching Principle

The objective is not to make students speak unnaturally fast.

The objective is:

smooth connected speech with natural word-to-word linking.

STAGE 6 — READING

Why are deadly extreme sports more popular than ever?

Time

18 minutes

Objective

Develop reading for gist, specific information, interpretation, and discussion.

PRE-READING

Show the four photographs.

Students identify:

  • bungee jumping;
  • paragliding;
  • skydiving;
  • wingsuit flying.

Ask:

Which looks most dangerous?

Why?

PREDICTION

Read the title:

Why are deadly extreme sports more popular than ever?

Ask:

Why do people do dangerous sports?

Students predict reasons.

FIRST READING — GIST

Instruction:

Read quickly. Find the reasons the writer gives for the growing popularity of extreme sports.

Students identify the relevant reasons.

SECOND READING — SPECIFIC INFORMATION

Students complete the missing-information task.

They look for:

  • names;
  • ages;
  • numbers;
  • percentages;
  • locations.

STRATEGY

Tell students:

Don't read every sentence slowly.

Instead:

QUESTION
 ↓
KEYWORD
 ↓
SCAN
 ↓
LOCATE
 ↓
CONFIRM

THIRD READING — INTERPRETATION

Return to the final paragraph.

Ask:

What does the writer mean by the idea that the future of extreme sports may be about learning to be less extreme?

Students discuss.

CRITICAL THINKING

Ask:

Do people become safer by becoming more extreme?

or:

Can controlled risk actually make people better at managing danger?

PERSONALIZATION

Students discuss:

Have you ever done an extreme sport?

Which would you most like to try?

Which would you never try?

Why?

STAGE 7 — VOCABULARY

Expressions with TAKE

Time

15 minutes

Objective

Teach take as a productive lexical system rather than as one isolated verb.

PART A — LISTENING / MATCHING

Students listen to Sophie Rees and match her answers to the questions.

Questions concern:

  • fear;
  • gender and extreme sports;
  • other extreme sports;
  • first extreme sport;
  • enjoyment;
  • growing popularity.

PART B — MISSING EXPRESSIONS

Students complete the expressions from the interview.

The key teaching point is:

take + complement often creates a new lexical meaning.

PART C — CONTEXTUAL MEANING

Work through selected expressions such as:

take care of
take advantage of
take place
take your time
take into account
take part in
take pity on
take off
take up
take on
take out
take out

Do not teach all meanings through translation.

Use:

expression
 ↓
context
 ↓
meaning
 ↓
own example

PART D — MEANING CHECK

For each selected expression ask:

Is this literal?

What does it mean here?

Can you replace it with another phrase?

Can you make your own sentence?

PART E — PERSONALIZATION

Students create sentences.

Examples:

I need to take into account...

I usually take part in...

I would like to take up...

I need to take out...

PART F — RETRIEVAL

Close the books.

Give students only:

take ______
take ______
take ______

They reconstruct expressions from memory.

STEP 5 — LANGUAGE ANALYSIS & TEACHING SUPPORT

5.1 ZERO CONDITIONAL

Form

if + present simple,
present simple

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you run the risk of paying expensive medical fees.

Meaning

The zero conditional presents a relationship as:

  • generally true;
  • habitual;
  • predictable;
  • logically connected.

It does not necessarily refer to one specific future event.

5.2 ZERO CONDITIONAL — CCQs

Target:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you run the risk of paying expensive medical fees.

Ask:

Is this only about one particular trip?

No.

Can it describe a general consequence?

Yes.

Does the speaker present the consequence as something that generally follows?

Yes.

5.3 FIRST CONDITIONAL

Form

if + present simple,
will + base verb

Example:

If you don't take out travel insurance, you will have to pay for the treatment yourself.

Meaning

The first conditional expresses:

a realistic / possible condition and its possible future consequence.

5.4 FIRST CONDITIONAL — CCQs

Is the situation possible?

Yes.

Is it necessarily going to happen?

No.

Is the consequence presented as a possible future result?

Yes.

5.5 ZERO vs FIRST CONDITIONAL

Zero Conditional First Conditional
general relationship possible future situation
present + present present + will
habitual / predictable possible consequence
If X happens, Y happens If X happens, Y will happen

5.6 IMPORTANT WARNING

Do not teach:

"zero = fact, first = future"

as an absolute rule.

The more useful distinction is:

How does the speaker conceptualize the relationship?

Is it:

a general / habitual relationship?

or:

a particular possible future situation?

5.7 FUTURE TIME CLAUSES

Common connectors:

when
as soon as
until
unless
before
after

Core Pattern

time connector
+
present simple
+
future meaning

Example:

When I arrive, I'll call you.

5.8 WHY PRESENT SIMPLE?

The present form occurs because English generally does not use will in the time clause to mark the future in this structure.

Correct:

When I arrive, I'll call you.

Incorrect:

When I will arrive, I'll call you.

5.9 FUTURE TIME CLAUSE — CCQs

Is the arrival in the future?

Yes.

Is "arrive" present simple?

Yes.

Is the main clause about the future?

Yes.

5.10 UNLESS

Teach:

unless = if ... not

Example:

You shouldn't go hiking alone unless someone knows where you are.

Conceptually:

unless = except if / if not

Avoid treating it as a simple synonym in every context; teach it through the sentence's actual meaning.

5.11 IN CASE

The textbook also uses in case in its speaking / grammar practice.

Important distinction:

in case expresses precaution against a possible future situation.

Example:

Take out travel insurance in case something goes wrong.

This is not simply identical to:

if.

The communicative idea is:

prepare now because a possible situation may happen later.

5.12 RISK LANGUAGE

Useful conceptual categories:

take a risk
run the risk of...
avoid a risk
reduce a risk
accept a risk
calculate a risk

Students should learn how the surrounding grammar changes.

For example:

run the risk of + -ing

not:

run the risk to + verb

5.13 LINKED PHRASES

Core Teaching Idea

Natural spoken English does not sound like a sequence of isolated dictionary words.

Instead:

word + word
   ↓
connected speech
   ↓
natural rhythm

Students should learn to recognize this rather than trying to pronounce every word separately.

5.16 EXPRESSIONS WITH TAKE

Core Principle

The meaning of take changes depending on the expression.

For example:

take care of
→ look after

take advantage of
→ use an opportunity / benefit from something

take place
→ happen

take your time
→ don't hurry

take into account
→ consider

take part in
→ participate

take pity on
→ feel sympathy and help

take off
→ leave the ground / remove clothing, depending on context

take up
→ begin a new activity / occupy space or time, depending on context

take on
→ accept responsibility / employ, depending on context

The lesson should emphasize:

expression first, isolated verb second.

5.18 READING ANALYSIS

The extreme-sports article provides a useful distinction between:

FACT
OPINION
EXAMPLE
EXPLANATION

Students should ask:

Is the writer giving evidence?

Is the writer explaining a reason?

Is the writer quoting someone?

Is the writer expressing a conclusion?

This turns reading into analysis rather than answer-hunting.

5.19 ARGUMENTATION LANGUAGE

For the writing stage, students can organize:

CLAIM
 ↓
REASON
 ↓
EXAMPLE
 ↓
CONSEQUENCE

Example pattern:

Extreme sports can be beneficial because they challenge people to control fear. For example, ...

Then the opposing side:

However, they can also be dangerous because...

5.20 FOR-AND-AGAINST THINKING

The key concept is balance.

Weak:

Extreme sports are great.

Stronger:

Extreme sports can provide challenge and excitement, but they also involve significant risks.

The goal is not to avoid having an opinion.

The goal is:

consider both sides before reaching a reasoned conclusion.

7.3 MAIN AIM

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to discuss risk and consequences using conditional structures, future time clauses, risk-related vocabulary, and expressions with take, and to express a balanced view of a risk-related issue.

7.4 SUB-AIMS

Students will:

  1. develop listening skills through risk-related interviews;
  2. develop speaking fluency through personal risk questions;
  3. distinguish zero and first conditionals;
  4. use future time clauses accurately;
  5. improve connected speech;
  6. read critically about extreme sports;
  7. expand their lexical knowledge of take expressions;
  8. practice balanced argumentation;
  9. understand video information about Grace Doyle and surfing.

7.5 ASSUMPTIONS

Students are assumed to:

  • know the basic present simple;
  • know the basic future with will;
  • have some experience discussing personal decisions;
  • have enough vocabulary to discuss everyday situations.

7.6 POTENTIAL PROBLEMS

Grammar Problem 1

Students may treat zero and first conditionals as interchangeable.

Solution

Use:

GENERAL RELATIONSHIP
vs.
POSSIBLE FUTURE

Grammar Problem 2

Students may put will in the if-clause.

Incorrect:

If I will go...

Correct:

If I go...

Grammar Problem 3

Students may put will after when / as soon as / until.

Incorrect:

When I will arrive...

Correct:

When I arrive...

Vocabulary Problem

Students may understand take only as the literal verb.

Solution

Teach the complete expression as a lexical unit.

Pronunciation Problem

Students may pronounce every word separately.

Solution

Model connected phrases and move from slow to natural speed.

Reading Problem

Students may translate every sentence.

Solution

Set clear reading purposes.

Listening Problem

Students may attempt to understand every word.

Solution

Use:

gist
 ↓
detail

Speaking Problem

Students may focus on accuracy and become silent.

Solution

Allow fluency first and use delayed correction.
